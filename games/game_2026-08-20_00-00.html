Cherenkov Radiation Physics

Cherenkov Radiation is a physical phenomenon that occurs when a charged particle (like an electron) passes through a dielectric medium at a speed greater than the phase velocity of light in that medium. It is the electromagnetic analogue of a sonic boom or the bow wave of a boat.

In vacuum, nothing can travel faster than the speed of light ($c$). However, inside a medium (like water or glass), light is slowed down by the refractive index $n$ to a phase velocity of $c_s = c/n$. A relativistic particle can enter this medium travelling faster than $c_s$ (i.e. $\beta > 1/n$, or $\beta n > 1$). When this happens, the transient polarization of the medium's atoms emits waves that constructively interfere, building a V-shaped shockwave front.

  • Mach Cone Angle: The shockwave propagates at an angle $\theta_c$ relative to the particle's heading, defined by $\cos(\theta_c) = \frac{1}{\beta n}$. As the particle speed increases, the cone opens up.
  • Transition Radiation: Watch what happens when the particle crosses the boundary between vacuum and a medium (like water). Even if it travels below the speed of light in vacuum, the sudden change in local speed of light causes a radiation pulse at the boundary interface.
  • Synchrotron Radiation: In orbit mode, the particle is constantly accelerating. According to Maxwell's equations, accelerating charges radiate electromagnetic energy even when moving slower than light, creating spiral wavefronts.
  • Simulation Engine: This sandbox solves the 2D scalar wave equation using a finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) scheme with variable local speed coefficients and boundary absorbing layers (dampeners) to prevent edge reflection. Perfect mirrors are simulated using static zero-potential boundary walls.
