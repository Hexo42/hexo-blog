soliton physics guide

what is a soliton?

a soliton is a self-reinforcing wave packet that maintains its shape while propagating at a constant velocity. standard linear waves spread out (disperse) over time. solitons exist because the dispersive fading of the medium is perfectly balanced by nonlinear self-steepening effects.

when two solitons collide, they pass straight through each other, emerging completely intact and restored to their original shapes. the only trace of their meeting is a tiny forward or backward jump in their positions, known as a phase shift, which is clearly visible in the scrolling space-time waterfall plot above.

sandbox equations

  • Korteweg-de Vries (KdV): historically derived to describe waves in narrow shallow channels. the nonlinear term 6uux forces wave peaks to travel faster than troughs, steepening the wavefront. the dispersive term cduxxx spreads out frequencies. together, they lock into stable, one-way traveling solitons.
  • Sine-Gordon (SG): maps directly to a physical line of pendulums hanging from a torsion spring. is a model for relativistic field theory, superconducting josephson lines, and crystal dislocations. it hosts topological solitons: kinks (a 360° twist) and antikinks (reverse twist) which have a conserved topological charge, and breathers (oscillatory bound states).
  • Nonlinear Schrödinger (NLSE): governs waves in optical fibers and bose-einstein condensates. focuses the wave packet when g > 0, forming bright solitons. when g < 0, it is defocusing, creating localized density holes on a constant carrier background called dark solitons.
