The Physics of Anderson Localization

In 1958, physicist Philip W. Anderson published a landmark paper showing that waves in a sufficiently disordered medium will cease to diffuse and instead become completely localized in space. This phenomenon, known as Anderson Localization, earned him the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physics.

While originally developed to explain how electrical conductivity drops to zero in disordered metals (an insulator transition), it is a fundamental wave phenomenon. It has been observed in acoustic waves, electromagnetic waves, and quantum matter waves (Bose-Einstein condensates in optical lattices).

How the Simulation Works

  • 2D Schrödinger Solver: The simulation integrates the time-dependent Schrödinger equation i ℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Hψ on an 80x80 tight-binding lattice using a symplectic staggered leapfrog integration method. This keeps the quantum mechanics stable at 60 fps.
  • Absorbing Boundary Conditions: Border grid cells contain an imaginary potential that acts as a sponge, damping outgoing probability current to prevent artificial reflections off the boundaries.
  • Lattice Disorder (Anderson vs. Speckle):
    • Anderson: A completely uncorrelated random potential energy value V(x,y) is assigned to each lattice node.
    • Speckle: The random potential is filtered with a spatial correlation Gaussian kernel, simulating optical speckle fields used in cold-atom experiments.
  • Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR): This quantum metric is defined as IPR = ∑ |ψ(x,y)|&sup4;. The Inverse of IPR (Participation Ratio, PR = 1 / IPR) measures the effective number of sites occupied by the wave. PR divided by the lattice size gives the Wave Coverage %. In clean lattices, Wave Coverage grows continuously; under localization, it hits a hard ceiling.
